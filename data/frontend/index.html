Supply & Demand Zones

Demand zones mark areas where price previously found buying interest; supply zones mark areas where selling pressure appeared. These candidate zones are inferred from the selected timeframe, not a measure of actual order flow.

How to understand these zones

A zone is a price band, not an exact line or an instruction to buy or sell. Read it alongside the chart and your own risk plan.

1

Start with the latest close

Compare the latest selected-timeframe close with each band. A zone above price is overhead; a zone below price is underneath; “inside” means the close is within the band.

2

Read the full band

The low and high are the zone boundaries. Price can move through part of a band before reacting, so don’t treat either boundary as a guaranteed turning point.

3

Look for confirmation

On a chart, observe how price behaves at the area. Some traders wait for a rejection or a candle close back away from the band; a touch alone is not confirmation.

4

Plan risk before any trade

Decide in advance what would invalidate your idea, how much you could lose, and whether the potential reward justifies that risk. No setup is certain.

What do “overlaps” and “distance” mean?

“Later candles overlapped” counts candle ranges on the selected timeframe that intersected the band after it formed. It does not count confirmed bounces or prove that buyers or sellers are still waiting there. “Distance” is the percentage between the latest close and the zone midpoint.

What makes a zone stronger or weaker?

This page does not assign a strength score. In general, traders may consider the move away from a zone, how recently it formed, repeated reactions, the broader trend, and whether price has closed through it. More revisits do not automatically mean a zone is stronger.

What are common mistakes?
  • Assuming a zone must hold or placing an order just because price touched it.
  • Confusing a historical price reaction with proof of current order flow.
  • Ignoring news, gaps, spread, timeframe, or the possibility of a break-through.
  • Risking money you cannot afford to lose or trading without an exit plan.
How are these zones calculated?

FinPulse looks for local highs and lows in selected-timeframe Finnhub candles, then uses part of the pivot candle’s range as a candidate band. A zone is omitted if a later candle close has crossed through it. This is a simple educational price-action method, not order-book or institutional-flow analysis.

Example: if a demand band is 95–98 and the latest close is 102, the band is below price. That only describes location; it does not mean price will fall to 98 or bounce there.
